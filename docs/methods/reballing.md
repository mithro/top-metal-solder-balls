# Reballing: getting the balls on

This is the easy half, and it is well-trodden hobby ground. It applies once
the pads are solderable, whether by [nickel](zincate-nickel.md),
[aluminium solder](aluminium-solder.md), or because the pads are the gold pads
of a [flex circuit](flex-ztape.md).

## How it works

- Use very little flux. Too much and the balls float and merge
  ([reball-guide]).
- Heat from below. Hot air from above blows 0.25 mm balls around
  ([badcaps]).
- Stencil apertures are cut slightly larger than the ball; one guide says
  about 5% ([reball-guide]).
- Stencil labels are not always accurate, so measure the apertures
  ([badcaps]).
- "Direct heat" stencils stay on the part during reflow and hold the balls
  in place.
- A hobbyist has hand-soldered a 12-ball, 0.4 mm pitch chip-scale package
  with a stencil and hot air, so handling parts this small at home is
  realistic ([mitxela]).
- Flux choice matters. One patent reports a proprietary flux giving bridging
  and zero yield on a hand-scale wafer-level ball attach ([flux-patent]).

## Design the die for the stencil

Off-the-shelf universal stencils pair 0.25 mm balls with 0.5 mm pitch. If the
die's pad array is drawn on a 0.5 mm grid, a $10 stencil fits it with no
custom tooling (my reasoning). On the full slot that allows 7 × 10 = 70 balls;
on the quarter slot 3 × 5 = 15, or 4 × 5 = 20 at about 0.46 mm with a custom
stencil.

## Lower temperature options

Since the parts only need to work to 50 °C, low-melting solder is fine and is
gentler on a marginal pad treatment. Tin-bismuth (Sn42Bi58, melts at 138 °C) is
sold as solder spheres including 0.25 mm ([bisn]). Indium-based solder
dissolves silver far more slowly than tin does ([ag-leaching]).

## Fit

**High.** Nothing here needs research. A die nest to hold a 2 to 5 mm die
under the stencil is the only thing to make.
