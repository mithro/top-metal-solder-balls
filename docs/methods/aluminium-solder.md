# Aluminium solder paste and flux, straight on the pad

**Idea.** Skip plating entirely. Special fluxes exist that strip aluminium
oxide at soldering temperature, and with a tin-silver or tin-zinc solder they
make a real joint to bare aluminium. One vendor sells this ready mixed as a
paste that can be printed through a stencil and reflowed on a hot plate.

## How it works

All of this comes from the flux vendor, Superior Flux, and all of it is about
bulk aluminium such as heat sinks.

- Tin alone "will not make a metallurgical bond to aluminum". Silver or zinc
  in the solder forms the bond once the oxide is gone ([superior-guide]).
- Their Direct Aluminum Soldering Paste uses 96.5/3.5 tin-silver and works by
  "induction, oven reflow, hot plate, or soldering iron". Cross-sections show
  a silver-aluminium intermetallic at the interface ([superior-paste]).
- Lap joints reached 882 psi aluminium to aluminium and 988 psi aluminium to
  copper, against 1138 psi copper to copper ([superior-paste]).
- The residue is water soluble: "cleaning involves simply rinsing in hot
  water" ([superior-paste]).
- The fluxes are active from 180 °C to 315 °C and soldering is usually done at
  240 °C to 290 °C. A low temperature paste (AL261-193-75) solders copper to
  aluminium at 180 °C to 240 °C. Heating has to finish within about 8 minutes or
  the flux is spent ([superior-guide]).
- "Aluminum soldering connections usually cannot survive exposure to salt
  water" ([superior-guide]). Not a concern at 0 to 50 °C indoors.

## Variants

- **Tin-zinc solder.** The traditional aluminium solder, because zinc is close
  to aluminium galvanically ([snzn-patent]). Zinc-rich phases corrode
  ([snzn-review]), which matters less at 0 to 50 °C.
- **Tinning paste first, balls second.** Superior also sells a tinning paste
  that is applied, heated quickly to 280 °C and rinsed off, leaving a surface
  that is easier to solder ([superior-guide]). Tin the pads, then
  [reball](reballing.md) with normal balls and normal flux.
- **Ultrasonic soldering iron.** Cavitation in the molten solder breaks the
  oxide without flux ([us-solder], [ohsu]). Solar cell makers tin evaporated
  aluminium this way ([ise-us]). The listings I found start around $2,000
  ([us-iron-price]), so this is not a hobby tool unless one turns up used.
  Aiming one at a 200 µm pad is a second problem.
- **Active solders** such as S-Bond and Cerasolzer bond to oxides directly
  with mechanical agitation ([sbond], [cerasolzer]). Same aiming problem.

## Known problems and unknowns

- **Whether a 1.1 µm pad survives.** The flux works by attacking the surface,
  and molten tin dissolves aluminium. On a heat sink fin nobody notices a few
  µm lost. Here that is the whole pad. This is the experiment to run.
- **Whether the flux attacks the passivation or creeps under it.** Aluminium
  fluxes are typically fluoride or amine based (my understanding; the vendor
  papers I read do not give the chemistry).
- **Residue under a 250 µm bump** on a die is harder to rinse than on a fin.

## Fit

**High as an experiment, unknown as a method.** If the pad survives, this is
by far the simplest route: three steps and no wet chemistry beyond a rinse.
