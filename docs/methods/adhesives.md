# Silver epoxy dots

This method puts a dot of silver-filled epoxy on each pad instead of a solder
ball, and glues the die face down to a board or [flex](flex-ztape.md).

## How it works

The steps below are unsourced and untested:

- Dispense or stencil-print the dots. 200 µm dots at 0.4 to 0.5 mm pitch are
  within reach of a laser-cut stencil.
- Break the oxide mechanically while the epoxy is wet, for example by
  scrubbing the die a few microns against the board, so that silver flakes
  touch fresh metal and the epoxy seals it from air.
- Put a noble surface on the pad first: [silver](silver.md) or
  [nickel](zincate-nickel.md).
- Design several pads per signal so one bad contact does not matter.

## Known problems and unknowns

- Aluminium's oxide is the problem. Bonding to it gives high initial
  resistance, and in moisture the silver and aluminium form a galvanic pair
  that grows more oxide ([meridian-al], [ica-aging]). Both sources are
  adhesive vendors.
- With anisotropic adhesive on bare aluminium pads, resistance rose during
  accelerated ageing because of oxide growth ([cityu-acf]).
- Industry therefore puts a [gold stud](../industry/stud-bump.md) on the pad
  first and dips the stud in epoxy ([quikpak]). That needs a wire bonder,
  which is out of scope here.

## Fit

**Medium.** This method needs no heat and no wet chemistry, and it is cheap.
Expect ohms, not milliohms, and drift over months. That is fine for slow I/O
at 0 to 50 °C and poor for power pins. The result is a glued-down die, not a
part that can be reflowed.
